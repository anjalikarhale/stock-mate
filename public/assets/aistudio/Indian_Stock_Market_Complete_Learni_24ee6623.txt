3. Beginner level: understand the Indian stock market
Module 1 — What is the stock market?
A stock market is a regulated marketplace where buyers and sellers trade shares and other financial instruments.

When you buy a company's share, you acquire a small ownership interest in that company. Your return can come from:
- Capital appreciation: The share price increases.
- Dividends: The company distributes part of its profits or reserves, subject to its policies and applicable rules.
- Losses: The share price falls, or the company performs poorly or fails.
Example: If you buy 10 shares at ₹200 each, your initial investment is ₹2,000 before costs. If the shares later trade at ₹250, their market value becomes ₹2,500, giving an unrealized gain of ₹500 before charges and taxes.

The important distinction: a higher share price does not automatically mean a company has become more valuable relative to its earnings, assets or future prospects.

Module 4 — How a stock trade works
A simplified transaction:
1. You select a stock and decide how many shares to buy.
2. You place a market order or limit order through your broker.
3. The exchange's matching system pairs compatible buy and sell orders.
4. Your order may be fully executed, partially executed or remain unfilled.
5. Clearing and settlement processes complete the transaction according to applicable rules.
Common order types:
Order	Meaning
Market order	Prioritizes execution at available prices, not a guaranteed exact price.
Limit order	Sets the maximum purchase price or minimum selling price you accept.
Stop-loss order	Activates or submits an order when its trigger condition is reached; execution price may differ from the trigger.
Stop-limit order	Uses a trigger and limit price; it may not execute.
GTT / conditional orders	Broker-specific features that act according to configured conditions and rules.

Learn the difference between the bid, ask, spread, liquidity, slippage and order-book depth. These matter especially when trading less-liquid shares.

Module 6 — The three financial statements
Every serious stock analysis should begin with the annual report, quarterly results, balance sheet, profit and loss statement, cash flow statement and notes to the accounts.









A. Profit and Loss Statement (P&L)

Shows revenue, expenses and profit over a period.
- Revenue: sales generated by the business.
- Operating profit: profit from operations before specified non-operating items and financing costs.
- Net profit: profit after expenses, interest and tax.
- EPS: earnings attributable to each share, subject to the reported EPS definition.
Question: Is the company growing profitably, or only increasing sales?












B. Balance Sheet

Shows assets, liabilities and shareholders' equity at a particular date.
- Assets: cash, inventory, property, receivables and other resources.
- Liabilities: loans, payables and other obligations.
- Equity: residual interest after liabilities.
Accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity.

Question: Can the company meet its obligations and fund future growth?












C. Cash Flow Statement

Tracks cash generated and spent.
- Operating cash flow (CFO): cash generated by business operations.
- Investing cash flow: capital expenditure, acquisitions and investment transactions.
- Financing cash flow: borrowing, debt repayment, share issuance, dividends and similar transactions.
Question: Does reported profit translate into actual cash generation?



A company can report accounting profits while facing cash-flow difficulties. That is why you must study all three statements together.
Module 7 — Financial ratios and formulas
These are core formulas for your project and practical stock screening.
Ratio	Formula	What it helps assess
EPS	Net income attributable to ordinary shareholders ÷ weighted average shares	Earnings per share
P/E	Market price per share ÷ EPS	Price paid for each rupee of earnings
P/B	Market price per share ÷ book value per share	Price relative to accounting net worth
ROE	Profit attributable to shareholders ÷ average shareholders' equity × 100	Return generated on equity
ROCE	EBIT ÷ average capital employed × 100	Return on operating capital
Debt-to-equity	Total debt ÷ shareholders' equity	Financial leverage
Current ratio	Current assets ÷ current liabilities	Short-term balance-sheet coverage
Operating margin	Operating profit ÷ revenue × 100	Operating profitability
Net margin	Net profit ÷ revenue × 100	Final profitability
Dividend yield	Annual dividend per share ÷ current share price × 100	Dividend income relative to price
Free cash flow	Operating cash flow − capital expenditure	Cash remaining after capital investment

Use consistent definitions when comparing companies. For example, some sources calculate ROCE using EBIT divided by average capital employed, while others use different conventions.

How to interpret ratios correctly
- A low P/E is not automatically a bargain; earnings may be declining.
- A high ROE can reflect strong operations, but it can also be amplified by debt or a small equity base.
- A low debt-to-equity ratio may be desirable for many businesses, but financial companies require sector-specific analysis.
- A high dividend yield may indicate a sustainable payout or a share price falling in anticipation of trouble.
- Compare ratios with industry peers and historical levels, not just a universal threshold.

Three valuation methods
1. Relative valuation

Compare a company's P/E, EV/EBITDA, P/B or other relevant multiples with comparable companies. Adjust for differences in growth, profitability, debt, accounting and business risk.

2. Discounted cash flow (DCF)

Estimate future free cash flows and discount them to present value:
\[
EV=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{FCF_t}{(1+r)^t}
+\frac{TV_n}{(1+r)^n}
\]

Here, \(r\) is the discount rate, \(TV_n\) is terminal value and \(EV\) is estimated enterprise value. To estimate equity value, adjust enterprise value for net debt and other relevant claims, then divide by the appropriate share count.

3. Dividend discount model

Estimate the present value of expected future dividends. A simplified constant-growth version is:
\[
P_0=\frac{D_1}{r-g}
\]

This model requires \(r>g\) and is most useful when dividends are reasonably predictable.
Practical valuation example
Suppose a hypothetical company has:
- EPS = ₹20
- Current market price = ₹400
- Your estimated fair P/E = 18
Its current P/E is \(400/20=20\). If the estimated fair P/E were justified, a simple implied value would be \(20\times18=₹360\).

That does not prove the share is worth ₹360. Your fair multiple is an assumption, and the calculation ignores changes in future earnings, risk, dividends and other factors. A credible valuation uses several scenarios rather than one precise-looking number.

Module 10 — Candlestick charts
A candlestick represents four prices for a selected period:
- Open: first traded price in the period.
- High: highest traded price.
- Low: lowest traded price.
- Close: last traded price.







sofi.com


A green or bullish candle conventionally means the close is above the open. A red or bearish candle means the close is below the open. The wicks show the range beyond the candle's body.
Candlestick patterns to learn









Hammer

A small body near the top with a long lower wick. It may suggest rejection of lower prices after a decline, but confirmation and context matter.











Bullish engulfing

A bullish candle's body engulfs the previous bearish candle's body. It may indicate improving buying pressure, particularly after a decline.











Doji

Open and close are very close. It can indicate indecision, but it does not independently predict a reversal.











Shooting star

A small body near the lower end of the candle's range with a long upper wick. It can signal rejection of higher prices after an advance.



Learn to interpret patterns near support, resistance and established trends rather than treating every candle as a standalone trading signal.
Module 11 — Trends, support and resistance

Three broad market conditions:
- Uptrend: higher highs and higher lows.
- Downtrend: lower highs and lower lows.
- Range-bound market: price oscillates between relatively stable support and resistance zones.
Support and resistance are better understood as zones than perfectly precise lines. A breakout can fail, and a level that previously acted as support can later become resistance.

Module 12 — Technical indicators









1. Moving averages (SMA and EMA)

Smooth price data and help identify trend direction. An SMA uses a simple average; an EMA gives greater weight to recent prices. Crossovers can lag and generate false signals.












2. Relative Strength Index (RSI)

Measures recent price momentum on a scale from 0 to 100. Readings above 70 or below 30 are conventional overbought/oversold reference zones, not automatic sell/buy signals.












3. MACD

The Moving Average Convergence Divergence indicator compares moving averages to examine momentum and trend changes. Crossovers and divergences need context.












4. Bollinger Bands

Place bands around a moving average using a volatility measure. Band expansion and contraction show changing volatility, but touching a band is not necessarily a reversal signal.












5. VWAP

Volume Weighted Average Price gives a volume-weighted average of traded prices over a defined session or anchored period. It is commonly used in intraday analysis.












6. ATR

Average True Range measures volatility, not direction. It can help calibrate stops and position sizes to changing market conditions.



Other tools to study include volume, OBV, stochastic oscillators, Fibonacci retracements, pivot points, ADX and Ichimoku. Start with a small, complementary set rather than adding many overlapping indicators.
Module 13 — Price action and market structure
Move beyond individual indicators to understand how prices develop.

Core concepts:
- Break of Structure (BOS): a break of a meaningful prior swing level, interpreted in the context of trend and timeframe.
- Change of Character (CHoCH): a possible early shift in directional structure; definitions vary between trading methodologies.
- Liquidity: the ability to trade without excessive price impact. In charting terminology, traders also use “liquidity” to refer to areas around obvious highs and lows where stop orders may cluster.
- Order blocks: chart zones interpreted by some traders as areas of prior institutional buying or selling. The institutional interpretation is not directly proven by the chart alone.
- Fair Value Gap (FVG): a three-candle price imbalance under a commonly used charting definition.
- Volume confirmation: comparing trading activity around breakouts, breakdowns and reversals.
Advanced terminology does not make a strategy profitable. Each concept needs an objective definition, repeatable rules and out-of-sample testing.
Module 14 — Volume, open interest and market participation
Understand the difference between the data types:
- Volume: quantity traded during a period.
- Open interest (OI): number of outstanding derivative contracts that remain open.
- Price and OI together: can help classify changes in participation, but do not reveal every participant's intent.
- PCR (put-call ratio): ratio of put activity to call activity, defined using either OI or volume. Those two versions are not interchangeable.
- Market breadth: the number or proportion of stocks rising versus falling.
- Advance-decline data: helps assess whether index movement is broad-based or concentrated in a few large stocks.
A common futures-market interpretation is:
Price	Open interest	Conventional interpretation
Rising	Rising	Long buildup
Falling	Rising	Short buildup
Rising	Falling	Short covering
Falling	Falling	Long unwinding

These labels are conventions, not proof of who initiated the trade. Every derivative contract has both a long and a short side. OI alone cannot tell you whether institutions are bullish or bearish.

6. Advanced level: derivatives, strategies and quantitative trading
Module 15 — Futures and options
Futures and options are derivatives: their values depend on an underlying instrument, such as an index or stock. They introduce leverage, expiry-related effects and additional risks.

Futures

A futures contract creates an obligation under its contract terms. Understand lot size, expiry, margin, mark-to-market settlement, basis and rollover.

Options

An option buyer pays a premium for a contract with specified rights. A call gives the buyer the right to buy the underlying under the contract terms; a put gives the buyer the right to sell. The option seller takes on corresponding obligations.

Learn these concepts:
- Strike price, spot price and premium.
- Expiry and time to expiry.
- Intrinsic value and time value.
- In-the-money, at-the-money and out-of-the-money options.
- Implied volatility and volatility skew.
- Exercise, assignment and settlement rules applicable to the contract.
- Margin, liquidity, bid-ask spread and expiry risk.
The options Greeks
Greek	What it measures
Delta	Sensitivity to a change in the underlying price
Gamma	Sensitivity of delta to a change in the underlying
Theta	Sensitivity to the passage of time
Vega	Sensitivity to changes in implied volatility
Rho	Sensitivity to changes in interest rates

Greeks are local sensitivity estimates, not guarantees. They change as the underlying, time and volatility change.

Example: Buying an option does not automatically make a trade safer than buying shares. The premium can lose most or all of its value, and short options can carry very large losses. Never choose an option solely because it looks cheap.
Module 16 — Trading systems and advanced analysis
Build competence in the following areas in order:
1. Market regime analysis: identify trending, ranging and high-volatility conditions.
2. Multi-timeframe analysis: distinguish the broad trend from the entry timeframe.
3. Strategy design: define objective entry, exit, stop and no-trade conditions.
4. Position sizing: calculate quantity from the risk allowed per trade.
5. Backtesting: test the rules on historical data while accounting for costs and realistic execution.
6. Forward testing: paper trade or trade in a simulated environment before risking meaningful capital.
7. Performance review: evaluate expectancy, drawdown, consistency and robustness.
8. Portfolio risk: measure correlated positions and total exposure, not just individual trade risk.
Advanced topics can include market profile, volume profile, anchored VWAP, systematic factor investing, statistical arbitrage, volatility models, algorithmic execution and Python-based research.

These are specializations, not prerequisites for basic market competence.
7. Risk management: the most important practical skill
A strategy can be correct often and still lose money if losses are too large. Risk management determines how much damage a bad trade can do.
Position sizing formula
\[
\text{Position size}=
\frac{\text{Maximum planned loss}}{\text{Entry price}-\text{Stop price}}
\]

Other risk concepts to master
- Risk/reward ratio: potential reward compared with planned risk.
- Expectancy: average expected profit or loss per trade over a sufficiently representative sample.
- Maximum drawdown: the largest peak-to-trough decline in portfolio value.
- Diversification: spreading exposure across different investments and risk factors.
- Correlation: how assets or positions move in relation to each other.
- Trading costs: brokerage, exchange charges, taxes, duties, slippage and other applicable costs.
- Leverage: exposure larger than the capital committed, which magnifies both gains and losses.
A high win rate alone is not evidence of a good strategy. Average wins, average losses, transaction costs and the risk of extreme losses matter too.

For perspective, SEBI reported that 93% of individual traders in the equity F&O segment incurred losses during FY2022–FY2024.

8. Build a complete trading strategy
A strategy should be a written set of rules, not a collection of chart signals selected after seeing what happened.

Here is an illustrative framework for a stock swing-trading system. It is a template to test, not a proven profitable strategy.
Example: trend-pullback system
1. Market filter: assess whether the benchmark and stock are in an established uptrend.
2. Stock selection: choose sufficiently liquid shares with acceptable business and financial risk.
3. Setup: wait for a pullback toward a defined trend reference or support zone.
4. Entry trigger: require a predefined bullish price-action confirmation instead of buying solely because the price fell.
5. Invalidation: determine the price or condition that would prove the setup wrong.
6. Position sizing: calculate quantity from the planned loss and available capital.
7. Exit: specify stop, profit-taking and time-based exit rules before entering.
8. Review: record the trade, costs, rule compliance and outcome.

Backtesting checklist
Before trusting a strategy, test:
- Different market regimes and multiple years of data.
- Out-of-sample periods not used to design the rules.
- Brokerage, taxes, slippage and realistic order fills.
- Survivorship bias, look-ahead bias and corporate actions.
- Maximum drawdown and losing streaks.
- Sensitivity to small changes in parameters.
- Performance against a simple benchmark.
A strategy that works only with one precise indicator setting or on a short historical period may be overfitted.
